## Why law matters in tech

Laws and technology shape each other. Understanding both helps you make informed decisions, keeps organizations honest, and helps governments write better policy. This lesson is an introduction, not legal advice.

## Case law

Courts create **precedent**: past decisions that guide similar future cases. Lawyers use cases to learn the law, predict what judges will do and build arguments. Many technology disputes settle before trial.

## The case brief

A **case brief** is a structured outline that gets you to the important parts fast:

- **Title and citation:** the parties and where the decision is published.
- **Facts:** what happened and why the parties are in court.
- **Issue:** the specific legal question, joining a legal principle to the facts (phrased as a question).
- **Decision and reasoning:** what the court ruled and how it got there, step by step.

## Think like a lawyer

Look at the whole story, step away from bias, examine the evidence, scrutinize sources, learn the rules, apply logic and argue from research. Widely repeated stories are often missing key facts.

## Walk-through: a case brief with an invented example

Real cases are long and use unfamiliar language, so a brief gives you a skeleton. Here is a made-up example so you can see the shape.

**Case:** *Rivera v. QuickShop Inc.* (fictional).

- **Facts:** QuickShop stored customers' passwords in plain text. Hackers stole the database. Rivera's accounts were then used to buy things. Rivera sued, saying QuickShop should have protected the data.
- **Issue:** Does a company that stores passwords in plain text breach its duty to protect customer data? (Notice the form: a **question** that joins a principle (duty to protect) to the facts (plain-text passwords).)
- **Decision:** Yes, QuickShop was liable.
- **Reasoning:** The court said that hashing passwords was a widely known, low-cost practice, so failing to use it was unreasonable. Because the harm was foreseeable, QuickShop owed Rivera protection.

**Using it later:** if another company is sued over the same kind of failure, lawyers point to *Rivera* as **precedent**: a past decision that guides similar cases.

## Steps for reading a real case

1. Read the **facts** first and say them back in your own words.
2. Find the **question** the judge is answering. It is often stated near the start.
3. Find the **holding** (the answer) and the **reasoning** (why).
4. Note anything the court said that was *not* needed to decide the case; it carries less weight.

## Thinking like a lawyer (practical habits)

Separate facts from opinions, ask who benefits from a story, check the original source instead of a headline, and write your argument with the evidence for each claim.
