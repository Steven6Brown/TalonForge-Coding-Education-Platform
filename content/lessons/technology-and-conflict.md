## Why security professionals study international relations

Cyber operations, disinformation and critical infrastructure attacks are tools of states and non-state actors, so understanding why conflicts happen helps you assess threats.

## Theories of conflict (briefly)

- **Realism:** the international system is anarchic (no world government); states are the key actors, can't be sure of each other's intentions, and seek survival. *Defensive* realists favor the status quo; *offensive* realists take opportunities to gain power.
- **Liberalism:** institutions and cooperation can reduce conflict.
- **Marxism:** focuses on the distribution of wealth.
- **Constructivism:** identities and social ideas shape behavior.

## Key concepts

- **Security dilemma:** steps one actor takes to be safer look threatening to others, leading to arms races and escalation.
- **Balance of power:** states try to prevent any one power from dominating. A **hegemon** is the dominant power; systems can be unipolar, bipolar or multipolar.
- **Balancing** (aligning against a threat) versus **bandwagoning** (aligning with it).

## Technology's effects

Technology changes the speed, scale and reach of diplomacy, intelligence and war; creates new issues that blur foreign and domestic lines; spreads information (and disinformation) through mass and social media; and diffuses capabilities such as drones and cyber tools to more actors.

## The future battlefield (framework)

Analysts look at **hardware** (connectivity, lethality, autonomy, sustainability), **software** (new concepts such as distributed and hybrid, non-kinetic warfare) and **users** (great powers, regional powers, non-state actors). More connectivity means more advantage and more targets, so adversaries try to disrupt it (for example, spoofing navigation signals).

## Key facts this lesson's quiz checks

- **Advantage and risk together.** Critical infrastructure and connectivity let **better information move faster**: the side that gets accurate information to decision makers quickly and securely gains an advantage. But that same dependence makes those networks a **target**: adversaries attack and disrupt them. The connectivity that creates the advantage is also the vulnerability.

## Worked example: advantage and vulnerability from the same cause

**Scenario.** A hospital network connects scanners, patient records, pharmacy systems and ambulances through one shared network.

**The advantage.** A doctor sees test results instantly, the pharmacy knows about an allergy before dispensing, and ambulance crews receive updated information while driving. Better information moving faster means better decisions.

**The vulnerability.** The same connectivity means that one successful attack, such as ransomware, can **stop everything at once**. An adversary does not need to defeat every system, only the network that joins them. Critical infrastructure (power, water, transport, health) is a target for exactly this reason.

**Pattern to remember:** the side that gets accurate information to decision makers **quickly and securely** gains the advantage, so protecting and disrupting that information flow becomes a goal in conflict.

## How defenders respond

- **Segment** the network so one failure does not spread everywhere.
- Keep **offline backups** and manual fallback procedures.
- **Monitor** for unusual activity and rehearse the response.
- Reduce dependence on a single link or supplier (**redundancy**).

## Technology as a tool of conflict, in short

Cyber operations are used for **espionage** (stealing information), **disruption** (denying service), and **influence** (shaping what people believe). They are cheap, deniable and fast compared with traditional force, which is why states and criminals both use them.
